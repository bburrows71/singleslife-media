Missing people is a real signal.

Researchers at MIT asked 40 healthy volunteers, mostly college students, to spend 10 hours alone in a windowless room. No phones, no seeing anyone. On a different day, the same people fasted for 10 hours.

Afterward, in a brain scanner, they looked at pictures of food, of people interacting, and of neutral things like flowers. After a day alone, pictures of people together activated a small midbrain area, the substantia nigra, in a way similar to how food pictures did after fasting. The stronger that activity, the more people said they were craving contact or food. In the senior researcher's words: "People who are forced to be isolated crave social interactions similarly to the way a hungry person craves food."

One honest limit: this was a single 10-hour lab stretch, mostly with students. It shows a signal, not a rule about anyone's life.

Here's the coaching part. A lot of us treat missing people as something to push through or keep quiet about. What if it's closer to hunger: information about a need, not a weakness?

Try it: this week, notice the moment you feel the pull toward someone. Instead of scrolling past it, ask: who am I actually hungry for? Then send that person one real message. Not a like. A sentence.

Source: MIT News, "A hunger for social contact" (Tomova, Saxe et al., Nature Neuroscience, 2020)
