Practice counts before it looks good.

Researchers at the University of Oxford taught juggling to adults who had never done it. 48 people, split in two groups. One group had weekly sessions for six weeks and practiced 30 minutes a day. The other had no training. Everyone had brain scans before and after.

The practice group showed changes in white matter, the brain's wiring, in areas involved in reaching and grasping. And the changes showed up in everyone who trained, no matter how well they ended up juggling. The researchers suggest it reflects the time spent practicing, not the skill level.

Two honest limits: the lead researcher notes MRI is an indirect measure, so they can't be sure exactly what changed. And she says this doesn't mean everyone should start juggling.

Here's the coaching part. Most of us judge new habits by how they look: the calm reply that still came out shaky, the hard conversation that went sideways. Practice may be doing something before the results show.

Try it: pick one thing you're working on. This week, keep a tally of how often you practice it, not how well it went.

Source: University of Oxford, "Juggling enhances connections in the brain" (Scholz, Johansen-Berg et al., Nature Neuroscience)
